THE BASICS

What Is Diabetes?

Understanding the metabolic condition affecting millions worldwide

Diabetes mellitus is a chronic metabolic disease characterized by high blood glucose levels, resulting from defects in insulin secretion, insulin action, or both.

Insulin is a hormone produced by beta cells in the pancreas. It acts as a "key" that unlocks cells, allowing glucose to enter and be converted into energy.

When the body cannot produce enough insulin or use it effectively, glucose accumulates in the bloodstream, progressively damaging blood vessels, nerves, and vital organs.

With proper treatment, people with diabetes can live fully normal, healthy lives.

Types of Diabetes

Each type has distinct causes, mechanisms, and treatment approaches — click any card to learn more

Type 1 Diabetes

~10% of cases

An autoimmune disease where the immune system attacks pancreatic beta cells, completely halting insulin production. Requires lifelong insulin therapy.

Common Questions
Can Type 1 diabetes be prevented?
No. Type 1 is an autoimmune condition — the immune system destroys insulin-producing cells. It is not caused by diet or lifestyle and currently cannot be prevented.
What is the main treatment for Type 1?
Lifelong insulin therapy — either through multiple daily injections or a continuous insulin pump. Regular blood glucose monitoring is also essential.
Can people with Type 1 live a normal life?
Absolutely. With proper insulin management, blood sugar monitoring, and a healthy lifestyle, people with Type 1 diabetes can live full, active, and long lives.

Type 2 Diabetes

~90% of cases

Characterized by insulin resistance combined with progressive secretion failure. Strongly linked to lifestyle and obesity — largely preventable.

Common Questions
Can Type 2 diabetes be reversed?
In many cases, yes. Losing 10–15% of body weight through diet and exercise can lead to full remission, especially when caught early. It requires sustained lifestyle changes.
What are the main risk factors?
Obesity, physical inactivity, poor diet, age over 45, family history of diabetes, and high blood pressure are the leading risk factors for developing Type 2 diabetes.
Does Type 2 always require medication?
Not always. Many people manage Type 2 diabetes through diet and exercise alone. However, some require oral medications or insulin as the condition progresses over time.

Gestational Diabetes

2–10% of pregnancies

Develops during pregnancy and usually resolves after delivery. Increases risk of Type 2 diabetes later in life by ~50%.

Common Questions
Does gestational diabetes harm the baby?
If uncontrolled, it can cause the baby to grow too large, leading to delivery complications. With proper management, most women with gestational diabetes have healthy pregnancies and babies.
Does it go away after delivery?
Usually yes — blood sugar typically returns to normal after birth. However, it significantly increases the mother's risk of developing Type 2 diabetes later in life.
How is gestational diabetes treated?
Through careful meal planning, physical activity, and blood glucose monitoring. Some women may also need insulin injections if diet and exercise alone are not sufficient to control blood sugar.

Nutrition Guide for Diabetics

Food is medicine — what you eat directly affects your blood sugar

Recommended — Low Glycemic Index

  • Leafy greens (spinach, broccoli, kale)
  • Fatty fish rich in omega-3 (salmon, sardines)
  • Eggs — excellent protein source
  • Raw nuts (almonds, walnuts)
  • Berries and strawberries
  • Whole oats, quinoa, whole grains
  • Lentils, chickpeas, beans

Avoid — Spike Blood Sugar Fast

  • Candy, cake, refined sugars
  • Soda, sports drinks, juices
  • White bread, white rice, pasta
  • Deep-fried, fast foods
  • Alcohol — causes dangerous swings
  • Milk chocolate, confections

Diabetes Myths — Fact or Fiction?

Correcting misconceptions with science

MYTH

Diabetes is caused by eating too much sugar

Type 1 is autoimmune, Type 2 is multifactorial — not just sugar intake alone.

FACT

Exercise can lower blood sugar without medication

Exercise activates muscle glucose uptake independently of insulin.

MYTH

Diabetics can never eat sweets

Small amounts can be enjoyed as part of a balanced plan.

FACT

Type 2 diabetes can go into remission

Losing 10-15% body weight may achieve full remission.

MYTH

Insulin is addictive

Insulin is a natural, essential hormone — not addictive.

FACT

Stress raises blood sugar

Cortisol from stress directly increases blood glucose levels.

About Diabetes
What is diabetes mellitus?
Diabetes mellitus is a chronic metabolic disease characterized by elevated blood glucose levels. It occurs when the body either does not produce enough insulin (Type 1) or cannot effectively use the insulin it produces (Type 2). Left untreated, it can cause serious complications affecting the heart, kidneys, eyes, and nerves.
What are the early warning signs of diabetes?
Common early signs include: frequent urination, excessive thirst, unexplained weight loss, constant fatigue, blurred vision, slow-healing wounds, and frequent infections. Many people with Type 2 diabetes have no symptoms initially — which is why regular screening is important.
What blood glucose level indicates diabetes?
According to the ADA: Fasting glucose ≥ 126 mg/dL, or 2-hour glucose ≥ 200 mg/dL during an oral glucose tolerance test, or HbA1c ≥ 6.5%, or random glucose ≥ 200 mg/dL with symptoms — any of these on two separate occasions confirms a diabetes diagnosis.
Is diabetes hereditary?
Both Type 1 and Type 2 have genetic components. If a parent has Type 2 diabetes, your risk is 40% higher. However, lifestyle factors play a major role — maintaining healthy weight and exercise habits can significantly reduce the genetic risk for Type 2 diabetes.
Can diabetes be cured permanently?
Type 1 currently has no cure. Type 2 can go into remission through significant weight loss (10–15% of body weight) and sustained lifestyle changes — meaning blood sugar returns to normal without medication. However, it requires lifelong healthy habits to maintain remission.
